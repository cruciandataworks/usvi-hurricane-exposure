Read with care

What these numbers can and cannot say

  • Early records are incomplete. HURDAT2 starts in 1851, but before aircraft reconnaissance (1944) and satellites (1960s) storms were only known from ships and land. Counts before then are a minimum.
  • Wind fields only exist from 2004. Before that HURDAT2 gives the centre and the strongest wind, not how far the winds reached, so older storms are measured by distance only.
  • Strength is the storm's, not the estate's. "Strength then" is the storm's maximum sustained wind near its centre. Winds on a given hillside depend on the side of the storm, terrain and exposure.
  • 2020 population for every storm. Residents and housing units are from the 2020 Census and are applied to past storms as a measure of exposure today.
  • Wind is not the only hazard. Rain, flooding, landslides and storm surge are not in this data.

How this was built

Two public datasets, one tested model, refreshed every three hours

NOAA's HURDAT2 database records every Atlantic storm's centre and strength every six hours since 1851. The pipeline fills each track in hour by hour, measures it against all 416 Census estates and, from 2004, checks which estates fell inside each storm's 34-, 50- and 64-knot wind radii by quadrant. During hurricane season it also reads the National Hurricane Center's live forecast for every active storm and opens a GitHub alert when one is forecast to pass within 150 nautical miles. Python lands the files into a Parquet lake, and DuckDB and dbt build every number on this page with tested SQL.
